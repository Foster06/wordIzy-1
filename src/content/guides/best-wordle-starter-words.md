Choosing the best Wordle starter word is the single most important decision you make each day. The right opening word can eliminate dozens of possibilities in one guess, set you up for a quick solve in two or three tries, and protect your win streak from devastating losses.

In this comprehensive guide, we break down the science behind Wordle starter words, rank the best options by strategy type, explain the math that separates great openers from mediocre ones, and give you a proven second-guess framework that works no matter what your first word reveals.

WHY YOUR STARTER WORD MATTERS MORE THAN YOU THINK

Wordle gives you six guesses to find a hidden five-letter word. After each guess, the game tells you which letters are correct and in the right position (green), which letters are in the word but in the wrong position (yellow), and which letters are not in the word at all (gray).

The information you get from your first guess is by far the most valuable because it narrows the search space the most. A well-chosen starter can eliminate 80 to 90 percent of all possible five-letter words in a single guess. A poorly chosen starter might only eliminate 30 to 40 percent, leaving you with a massive pool of candidates and very few guesses left.

Researchers at MIT published a study analyzing all 12,972 accepted five-letter Wordle answers using information theory. They found that the optimal starting word, measured by average information gain across the entire answer pool, is SALET — it consistently reduces the candidate pool to its smallest expected size after one guess.

THE SCIENCE: WHAT MAKES A GREAT STARTER WORD

Three principles separate elite Wordle starters from average ones.

First, vowel coverage. English words are vowel-heavy. The five standard vowels (A, E, I, O, U) appear in the vast majority of five-letter answers. A starter word that includes two or three different vowels gives you immediate signal on which vowels are present, which are absent, and roughly where they sit. Words like ADIEU (four vowels) or AUDIO (four vowels) are popular for this reason — they test many vowels at once.

Second, consonant frequency. Not all consonants are equally likely. The most common consonants in five-letter Wordle answers are R, S, T, L, N, and C. A great starter word includes at least one or two of these high-frequency consonants alongside its vowels. This is why CRANE, SLATE, and TRACE outperform vowel-heavy words in practice — they balance vowel coverage with consonant information.

Third, positional probability. Some letters are far more likely to appear in certain positions. For example, S is the most common starting letter in five-letter answers, E is the most common ending letter, and A frequently appears in the middle. A word like SLATE puts high-frequency letters in their most common positions, maximizing the chance of landing green tiles on the first guess.

Here are the ten best starter words based on data from millions of Wordle games, ranked by average number of guesses to solve.

1. SALET — The mathematically optimal opener. Tests S (most common start), A and E (most common vowels), L and T (high-frequency consonants). Average guesses to solve: 3.42.

2. CRANE — The fan favorite. Tests C, R, A, N, E — all top-10 letters. The C and R combination is especially powerful because it appears in hundreds of answers. Average guesses to solve: 3.45.

3. SLATE — S start, L and T consonants, A and E vowels. Nearly identical performance to SALET. Average guesses to solve: 3.46.

4. CRATE — C, R, A, T, E. Very similar to CRANE but swaps N for T, which is slightly more common. Average guesses to solve: 3.48.

5. TRACE — T, R, A, C, E. A strong alternative if you want T in the first position. Average guesses to solve: 3.50.

6. ADIEU — The vowel-maximal approach. Tests four of five vowels (A, E, I, U). Great for vowel information but weak on consonants. Average guesses to solve: 3.54.

7. AUDIO — Tests A, U, I, O — four vowels including the less common U and O. Slightly worse than ADIEU because D is less useful than E. Average guesses to solve: 3.56.

8. RAISE — R, A, I, S, E. Covers three vowels and two high-frequency consonants. A solid all-rounder. Average guesses to solve: 3.57.

9. SOARE — S, O, A, R, E. Another balanced option with S at the start. Average guesses to solve: 3.58.

10. IRATE — I, R, A, T, E. Three vowels plus R and T. A good choice if you want to test I early. Average guesses to solve: 3.60.

STRATEGY 1: THE VOWEL-FIRST APPROACH

The vowel-first strategy prioritizes identifying which vowels are in the answer as quickly as possible. The logic is simple: knowing the vowels narrows the answer pool dramatically because most five-letter words have two or three vowels.

The best vowel-first starters are ADIEU and AUDIO. Both test four different vowels in a single guess. If you play ADIEU and get:
- All gray: the answer has no A, D, I, E, or U. This is extremely rare but immediately narrows the pool to words with only O as a vowel (like BLOCK, FROST, GHOST).
- One yellow: you know one vowel and can focus your second guess on words containing it.
- One green: you know one vowel and its position, which is very powerful.

The weakness of the vowel-first approach is that you learn very little about consonants. If ADIEU returns all gray, you still have no information about the consonants in the answer, which means your second guess needs to do double duty.

Strategy 1 is best for players who struggle with vowel identification and want a systematic way to eliminate vowel possibilities early.

STRATEGY 2: THE BALANCED APPROACH

The balanced strategy uses a starter word that includes two vowels and three high-frequency consonants. This is the approach used by CRANE, SLATE, TRACE, and RAISE.

The advantage of the balanced approach is that you get information about both vowels and consonants simultaneously. A word like CRANE tests:
- Two vowels (A, E) — the two most common vowels in English
- Three consonants (C, R, N) — all in the top 10 most frequent consonants

If CRANE returns, say, green-A and yellow-R, you immediately know:
- The answer has A in position 3
- The answer has R somewhere (likely position 1 or 2)
- The answer does not have C, N, or E

This is a massive amount of information from a single guess, and it lets you make a highly informed second guess.

The balanced approach is recommended for most players because it maximizes expected information across all possible answer patterns.

STRATEGY 3: THE CONSONANT-HEAVY APPROACH

Some advanced players prefer a consonant-heavy opener like STERN, THORN, or CLINT. These words test three or four high-frequency consonants and only one vowel.

The logic here is that consonants carry more positional information than vowels. A green S in position 1 is far more informative than a green A in position 3 because S-starting words are a much smaller subset than A-containing words.

The downside of the consonant-heavy approach is that it can leave you guessing about vowels for longer, which sometimes results in needing four or five guesses instead of three. However, for expert players who can quickly identify vowel patterns from consonant information, this approach can be very effective.

THE PERFECT SECOND GUESS: A FRAMEWORK FOR EVERY SCENARIO

Your second guess is almost as important as your first. Here is a framework for choosing your second word based on what your starter revealed.

SCENARIO A: All five letters gray (complete miss). This is rare but devastating. Your second word should test an entirely different set of high-frequency letters. If your starter was CRANE, good second guesses include: GHOST, BLIMP, JUMPY, FUDGE. Pick the word that tests the most new high-frequency letters.

SCENARIO B: One or two yellow letters, no green. You know which letters are in the word but not where. Your second guess should reposition those letters into their most likely positions while also testing new letters. For example, if CRANE gave you yellow-A and yellow-R, try TRADE or GRATE — both reposition A and R while testing new letters.

SCENARIO C: One green letter, rest gray. You know one letter and its position. Your second guess should build around that green letter. If CRANE gave you green-A (position 3), try FLASK, PLANT, or CHARM — all place A in position 3 and test new consonants.

SCENARIO D: Two green letters. You are in excellent shape. Focus on filling the remaining three positions with words that match the pattern. If CRANE gave you green-C and green-A (C_A__), try CLEAR, COAST, or COULD.

SCENARIO E: One green and one or more yellow. This is the most common outcome and the most complex. Prioritize the green letter, reposition the yellow letters, and fill remaining slots with new high-frequency letters.

ADVANCED TIPS FOR EXPERT PLAYERS

1. Avoid repeating gray letters. This sounds obvious, but under time pressure players often accidentally reuse a letter they already know is absent. Always double-check your second and third guesses against the gray letters from previous guesses.

2. Consider hard mode carefully. In hard mode, you must reuse any revealed green or yellow letters in subsequent guesses. This makes the game significantly harder because you cannot freely test new letters. If you play hard mode, your starter word matters even more because you are locked into its revealed letters.

3. Track letter frequency in the answer pool. The official Wordle answer list has 2,309 words (not the 12,972 accepted guesses). The answer pool is curated to avoid obscure words, which means certain letters appear more frequently than in the general English corpus. S is the most common starting letter (366 words), E is the most common ending letter (422 words), and A is the most common middle letter.

4. Use process of elimination for your last guess. If you are down to two possible answers and have one guess left, do not waste it testing a third option. Pick one of the two and commit. You have a 50 percent chance of winning, which is better than the 33 percent chance of testing a third word.

5. Watch for double letters. About 7 percent of Wordle answers contain a repeated letter (like LLAMA, OTTER, or SASSY). If your guesses keep revealing that common letters are absent, consider that the answer might have a double letter, which would explain why certain positions are not matching.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

1. Starting with the same word every day without thinking. While having a go-to starter is fine, blindly repeating the same second and third guesses regardless of the first-guess feedback is the fastest way to lose your streak.

2. Wasting guesses on obscure words. Guessing XYLYL or PZAZZ might seem clever, but these words test almost no useful letters and waste a precious guess. Stick to words with common letters.

3. Ignoring positional information. A yellow S is very different from a green S. A yellow S tells you S is in the word but not in position 1, which means it is likely in position 4 or 5 (as a suffix like -SS or -ST). Use positional logic to place letters efficiently.

4. Guessing too aggressively early. If you have three possible answers and four guesses left, do not guess randomly. Use your extra guesses to test letters that differentiate between the three possibilities. For example, if the three options are BATCH, CATCH, and HATCH, guess a word with B, C, and H (like BEACH) to determine which one it is.
